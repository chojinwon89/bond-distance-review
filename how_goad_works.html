How GOAD finds the lowest-energy structure

A genetic-algorithm global search over a compact adsorbate genome — and why that solves a different problem than a DFT geometry optimization, which relaxes locally to the nearest minimum.
← gallery · binding energy · structure & geometry · method validation · how GOAD works · single-point DFT vs ML · SevenNet vs MatterSim · likely failures

This page explains the search strategy behind the GOAD + SevenNet-OMNI structures in this benchmark: what GOAD actually optimizes, how the genetic algorithm (GA) converges on the lowest-energy adsorption geometry, the exact equations it uses, and how all of that differs from a DFT relaxation that “rolls downhill” on the potential-energy surface (PES).

Grounded in the engine source goad_v1/ga/genetic_algorithm.py. Full write-up: GOAD_ALGORITHM.md · engine app: GOAD_ENGINE.md.

1. The problem: one PES, many valleys

Put a molecule near a surface and its energy is a function of all the atomic coordinates — the potential-energy surface (PES). For an adsorbate this is not a simple bowl but a rugged landscape with many minima, because the molecule can sit at different sites (atop / bridge / hollow), in different orientations, and in different conformations:

 E
 |   \          /\            /\
 |    \        /  \    /\    /  \        <- each valley = one locally stable
 |     \  /\  /    \  /  \  /    \          adsorption geometry (a local minimum)
 |      \/  \/      \/    \/      \___
 |                        ^
 |                        +-- global minimum = the structure we actually want
 +------------------------------------------> configuration (site, orientation, torsion...)

The global minimum is the physically correct, most stable structure. The difficulty is that valleys are separated by energy barriers: you cannot reach the deepest valley from a random start just by going downhill, because a downhill path stops at the bottom of whatever valley you happen to start in.

2. What GOAD optimizes: a compact “genome”, not raw coordinates

GOAD does not search all $3M$ Cartesian coordinates. It fixes the surface and treats the molecule as a semi-rigid body, encoding each candidate placement as a short vector — the genome:

3 position — centre-of-mass $(x,y,z)$
3 orientation — ZYX Euler $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$
N torsions — one dihedral per rotatable bond

So a butanol placement is ~9 numbers, not ~45 Cartesian coordinates. This small, chemically meaningful space of dimension $6+N$ is what makes a global search affordable. Generation 0 is biased, not random: the reactive atom is seeded near its known bond distance (lowest O at ~2.3 Å, else lowest C at ~2.1 Å above the surface), and each candidate is built as apply torsions → centre → rotate → translate.

Fitness = adsorption energy from a fast MLIP

Each candidate is scored by a single-point adsorption energy (no relaxation) with the surface atoms fixed (FixAtoms), using a machine-learning interatomic potential (MLIP) — SevenNet-OMNI here (the engine also supports MatterSim / CHGNet / MACE). One MLIP energy is milliseconds-to-seconds; one DFT energy is minutes-to-hours — that speed is what lets GOAD score thousands of candidates. Lower is fitter.

3. The genetic-algorithm loop

The GA keeps a population of placements (default 30) and improves them over generations (up to 200) by mimicking natural selection:

flowchart TD
    A["Initialize population
(random x,y,orientation,torsions;
reactive atom biased toward surface)"] --> B["Evaluate fitness
E_ads for every individual (MLIP, surface fixed)"]
    B --> C{"Improved in last
30 generations?"}
    C -->|"no (< 0.001 eV)"| Z["Stop early → return best structure"]
    C -->|yes| D["Elitism: keep best 5 unchanged"]
    D --> E["Tournament selection
(pick 5, best one wins → parent)"]
    E --> F{"crossover?
(rate 0.7)"}
    F -->|yes| G["Crossover: position from parent A,
orientation from parent B, torsions mixed"]
    F -->|no| H["Mutate one gene block:
position / orientation / torsions"]
    G --> I["New generation"]
    H --> I
    I --> B
    Z --> R["Post-GA local relaxation (BFGS, fmax=0.02 eV/Å)
→ final adsorption structure"]

The operators are what make this a global search rather than a downhill walk. Tournament selection + elitism drive the population toward low energy (exploitation); crossover recombines parts of two good solutions (site of one, orientation of another) and can land in a valley neither parent occupied; mutation nudges position, orientation, or torsions and can hop over a barrier (exploration). The GA returns the best genome — the right basin. A final local BFGS relaxation then settles every atom to that basin’s exact bottom. Global search first, local refinement second.

4. The equations GOAD actually uses

Everything above reduces to a handful of formulas that live in the engine.

(1) The objective — a global minimization over the genome

$$\mathbf{g}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{g}}\;E_{\text{ads}}(\mathbf{g}),\qquad \mathbf{g}=(x,y,z,\;\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\;\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{6+N}.$$

Dimensionality is $6+N$, not $3M$ for $M$ atoms — that reduction is what makes a global search affordable.

(2) The fitness — single-point adsorption energy, surface fixed

$$E_{\text{ads}}=E_{\text{slab+mol}}-E_{\text{slab}}-E_{\text{mol}}.$$

Evaluated once per candidate with FixAtoms on every slab atom. $E_{\text{slab}}$ and $E_{\text{mol}}$ are precomputed constants, so scoring costs one MLIP energy call.

(3) Genome → coordinates — the rigid-body placement operator

$$\mathbf{r}_i' = R(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\,(\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_{\text{com}})+\mathbf{t},\qquad \mathbf{t}=(x,y,z),\qquad R=R_z(\gamma)\,R_y(\beta)\,R_x(\alpha).$$

Centre the molecule, apply the ZYX Euler rotation about the centre of mass, then translate to $(x,y,z)$.

(4) Chemically-biased initialization

$$\min_i z_i^{\text{reactive}} = z_{\text{surf}}^{\max}+d,\qquad d=2.3\ \text{Å}\ \text{(if O present)},\quad 2.1\ \text{Å}\ \text{(otherwise)}.$$

In generation 0 the height is set so the lowest reactive atom sits at its known bond distance above the top surface layer $z_{\text{surf}}^{\max}$.

(5) Selection + elitism

$$\text{parent}=\arg\min_{i\in\mathcal{T}}E_i,\quad \mathcal{T}\subset\{1,\dots,P\},\;|\mathcal{T}|=5;\qquad \text{keep top } e=5 \text{ unchanged}.$$

Parents come from a size-5 tournament (fittest of five random candidates); the best five individuals survive each generation untouched.

(6) Crossover — applied with probability $p_c=0.7$

$$\mathbf{p}_c=\mathbf{p}_A,\qquad \boldsymbol{\omega}_c=\boldsymbol{\omega}_B,\qquad \theta_i^{\,c}=\theta_i^{A}\ \text{if } u\lt 0.5\ \text{else}\ \theta_i^{B},\quad u\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1).$$

The child takes position from parent $A$, orientation from parent $B$, and each torsion from either parent by a coin flip.

(7) Mutation — applied with probability $1-p_c=0.3$ (one gene block)

$$\mathbf{p}\leftarrow \mathbf{p}+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_p^2\mathbf{I}),\ \sigma_p=0.5\ \text{Å};\qquad \boldsymbol{\omega}\leftarrow(\boldsymbol{\omega}+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_o^2\mathbf{I}))\bmod 360^\circ,\ \sigma_o=10^\circ;$$
$$\theta_i\leftarrow(\theta_i+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_t^2))\bmod 360^\circ,\ \sigma_t=20^\circ;\qquad z\leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\!\big(z,\ z_{\text{surf}}^{\max}+b,\ z_{\text{surf}}^{\max}+h_{\max}\big).$$

One block (position / orientation / torsions) is nudged by Gaussian noise; the height is clamped back into the interaction window after every move.

(8) Early stopping

$$\text{stop when } E_{\text{best}}\ \text{fails to improve by}\ \gt\tau\ \text{for } 30 \text{ consecutive generations},\quad \tau=10^{-3}\ \text{eV}.$$

(9) Post-GA local relaxation (the only force-based step)

$$F_{\max}=\max_i\lVert \mathbf{f}_i\rVert=\max_i\lVert-\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_i}E\rVert\le 0.02\ \text{eV/Å}.$$

The GA winner is relaxed with BFGS until the largest atomic force falls below threshold — a local move that finds the exact bottom of the basin the GA selected.

Default constants (all overridable): population $P=30$, generations $\le 200$, $p_c=0.7$, elite $e=5$, tournament $=5$, $\sigma_p=0.5$ Å, $\sigma_o=10^\circ$, $\sigma_t=20^\circ$, early-stop 30 gens / $10^{-3}$ eV, BFGS $F_{\max}=0.02$ eV/Å.

5. How this differs from a DFT geometry optimization

A DFT “relaxation” is a local optimizer. From a starting structure $\mathbf{x}_0$ it computes forces (the gradient of the PES, from solving the Kohn–Sham equations) and steps atoms downhill until they vanish:

$$\mathbf{x}_0 \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{follow } -\nabla E\ }\; \mathbf{x}^{\ast}\quad\text{with}\quad \nabla E(\mathbf{x}^{\ast})=0,$$

which is the nearest local minimum — the bottom of whatever valley the starting guess was already in. It never crosses a barrier. GOAD’s objective, by contrast, is the global $\arg\min$ over the whole landscape.

 Local (DFT relaxation): one ball, released once, rolls to the nearest bottom.

        start o                         start o
               \                               \
     /\        _\/          vs.       /\        _\/        <- both stop here, in
    /  \      /            depends    /  \      /             whatever local min
   /    \____/             on where  /    \____/              was closest; the deeper
        ^ nearest local min          valley on the right is never found.

 Global (GOAD GA): a whole population scattered across the landscape, bred toward
 the deepest valley -- so the global minimum IS discovered, then relaxed locally.

There are really two independent differences — the search strategy and the energy source:

DFT optimization answers “what is the exact bottom of this valley?” GOAD answers “which valley is the deepest?” They are complementary: a DFT relaxation is only as good as the structure you feed it — start it in the wrong valley and it faithfully converges to the wrong local minimum.

6. Why this matters for the benchmark

The whole pipeline is: GOAD’s GA (global search, MLIP energies) picks the basin → a local relaxation settles the geometry → DFT is the reference of record. This benchmark measures whether that fast global search lands in the same valley DFT would, and whether the MLIP energies track the DFT energies. If GOAD + MLIP reproduces the DFT global-minimum geometry (bond distance, adsorption site) and energy ranking, the cheap global search can stand in for exhaustive DFT structure-searching — DFT still supplies the accurate final energies, GOAD removes the need to guess which structure to compute.

See the method-validation page for the quantitative version of this argument (geometry parity, site-prediction accuracy, energy-ranking correlation, and screening efficiency).

One-paragraph summary

GOAD encodes an adsorbate placement as a short genome — surface position, orientation, and one dihedral per rotatable bond — and runs a genetic algorithm that scores candidates by their MLIP adsorption energy (single-point, surface fixed), using tournament selection, elitism, crossover, and mutation to breed the population toward the lowest-energy basin of the PES, then relaxes the winner locally with BFGS. A DFT geometry optimization, by contrast, is a purely local move: it follows first-principles forces downhill from a single starting structure to the nearest minimum and cannot escape that valley. GOAD does the global search (which structure?) with cheap energies; DFT provides the local, accurate reference (how deep, exactly?).
